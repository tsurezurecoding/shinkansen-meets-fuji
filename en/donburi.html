Japan, one rice bowl at a time.

From an easy beef bowl to a meal worth making time for.

A warm lunch between sights. Chicken and soft egg after a morning on your feet. Seafood or grilled eel to plan an afternoon around. Get to know Japan’s rice bowls, and your next meal becomes part of the adventure.

A temple visit turns into a long walk. Lunch slips past, and your dinner reservation is still hours away. This is when recognising a restaurant sign can be as useful as knowing your next train.

Look out for Yoshinoya, Matsuya and Sukiya. These casual chains are handy places to sit down to a warm meal, including when you’re travelling alone. Knowing a few reliable names lets you leave some meals open and follow the day wherever it leads.

The dish to know is gyudon: a beef and onion rice bowl. Thin slices of beef and onion simmer in a lightly sweet, savoury sauce, then go over the rice. It belongs to a larger family called donburi, or rice bowls with toppings. Pork cutlets, chicken and egg, seafood and eel all have their own place in that family.

01 / An easy meal along the way

Start with a sign you recognise.

A plain, regular beef bowl is an easy first order. Take a bite of beef, sweet onion and rice together, then try a little of the pickled ginger or chilli seasoning if you like. You can adjust the flavour as you eat.

All three chains serve a version of this simple meal. Choose a convenient branch along your route. Once you’ve tried the basic bowl, the toppings on the menu will give you plenty of ideas for a return visit.

Know the name from home? Try it here, too.

Yoshinoya also has restaurants in the United States, Singapore, Taiwan, Hong Kong and other parts of Asia. If you already know the name, try its Japanese menu: begin with the plain beef bowl, then choose your own egg, ginger or topping combination.

A reference for travellers from Singapore: on 15 November 2026, the official menus listed a regular beef bowl at ¥498 including tax for eating in in Japan and a regular gyudon at S$6.50 in Singapore. These are separate menus: portions, included items, local taxes and exchange rates affect the comparison. Check both current menus when budgeting.

A useful breakfast option, too.

If your hotel stay doesn’t include breakfast, find a nearby branch the night before. Some offer breakfast sets with eggs or grilled fish as well as beef bowls. Check that your chosen branch opens in time, and allow for the walk from the restaurant to your train platform.

02 / Your first order

Three choices get you started.

Choose eat in or takeaway, your bowl, then its size. “Eat in, beef bowl, regular” is a useful starting point. At Matsuya, look for gyumeshi, its name for the beef bowl. Namimori means a regular serving; omori means large. For a smaller meal, look for a small or mini option.

Ordering with a member of staff?

“Gyūdon, namimori o hitotsu.”

牛丼、並盛をひとつ。

“One regular beef bowl, please.”
At Matsuya, use “gyūmeshi” in place of “gyūdon”. Pointing to the menu photo works, too.

If there’s a meal ticket machine by the entrance, order and pay there first. Choose English if it’s offered, then select eat in or takeaway, your meal and its size. Keep the ticket. At a self-service branch, watch the collection screen and take your ticket to the counter when your number is ready. At other branches, you hand the ticket to a member of staff.

You may instead order on a tablet at your seat or directly with staff. The system can vary even within the same chain, so follow the signs at the entrance. If there’s a tray return area, take your dishes there when you’ve finished.

A set adds side dishes such as soup or salad; check what’s included. An egg in the menu photo may be an extra. Egg options can include a raw egg or an onsen tamago, a very softly cooked egg.

See Matsuya’s illustrated guides to ticket machines and self-service branches (English PDFs).

A few words to make it your own.

After your first bowl, you may know just what you’d change: more sauce soaking into the rice, perhaps, or a little more beef. These words help you ask for it.

Tsuyudaku つゆだく
More of the sauce used to simmer the beef, ladled over your rice. Try “Gyūdon, namimori, tsuyudaku de” for a regular beef bowl with extra sauce.
Atama no ōmori アタマの大盛
Regular rice with a larger helping of beef and onions. This is a portion size on Yoshinoya’s menu. Here, atama refers to the topping that sits above the rice.
Nikudaku 肉だく
An additional serving of the beef and onion topping. At Yoshinoya, the extra portion arrives in a separate small bowl. A good option when it’s the beef you want more of.

Atama no ōmori is a size; nikudaku is an extra topping. Names, availability and ways to request more sauce vary by restaurant. If you can’t find an option on the screen, ask staff while ordering.

Yoshinoya explains daku, atama no ōmori and its current nikudaku bowl on its official site (Japanese).

Cheese today. Something tangy next time.

The toppings make a second visit tempting. Cheese adds richness to the beef. For a little heat, look for mentaiko, chilli-seasoned fish roe, and takana, pickled mustard greens. They sometimes appear together, or with a mentaiko-flavoured mayonnaise sauce.

For a tangier bowl, try oroshi ponzu: grated daikon radish with a citrusy sauce. Once you know a few of these names, even a familiar menu starts offering new possibilities.

Toppings vary by restaurant and season. Browse the official English menus: Yoshinoya, Sukiya and Matsuya.

03 / Your next bowl

Meet chicken, egg and a very good pork cutlet.

Once you’ve met gyudon, try another member of the donburi family. Two easy names to remember are katsudon and oyakodon. Both bring egg and a savoury, lightly sweet cooking sauce to the rice, but the meat gives each a different character.

Katsudon かつ丼

A breaded pork cutlet, simmered with egg and served over rice. The coating soaks up the sauce, making this a satisfying lunch after a long walk. Try a chain such as Katsuya (Japanese menu), or look for it at a soba noodle restaurant. Sauce katsudon is another version, dressed with sauce rather than cooked with egg; the menu photo helps you tell them apart.

Oyakodon 親子丼

Chicken and egg, gently cooked together over rice. The name means “parent and child”, a reference to the two main ingredients. It’s a comforting choice when you feel like something soft and warm. Nakau (Japanese menu) is a convenient chain to look for; chicken specialists serve it too. The egg may be softly set, and some versions add an egg yolk on top.

Love tempura? Look for tendon, pronounced “ten-don”: a rice bowl topped with tempura such as prawns and vegetables. Tenya’s menu (Japanese) shows different combinations. A change of topping takes the familiar bowl in a completely different direction.

04 / A meal to plan around

Save one lunch for seafood or eel.

Some bowls are a convenient stop along the way. Others give you a reason to visit a neighbourhood. A tuna specialist, a seafood lunch near a market, a restaurant grilling eel: find one that catches your imagination, then build a little exploring around the meal.

Let tuna take centre stage.

Magurodon · Tuna rice bowl

If tuna is your favourite part of a sushi meal, give it a bowl of its own. Maguro means tuna. Lean red slices, richer fatty cuts and zuke, tuna marinated in a soy-based sauce, offer different ways to enjoy it.

Tsukiji is a food neighbourhood in central Tokyo, where the narrow streets of the Outer Market are lined with seafood restaurants and food shops. The wholesale fish market moved to Toyosu in 2018; Tsukiji’s Outer Market remains a place to explore and eat. Visit for a morning stroll and stay for lunch.

A specialist such as Seagen (Japanese site) makes the bowl itself the reason to visit. Look at the cuts and portion sizes on the menu and choose the one you most want to taste.

Seagen serves lunch. Check its opening days and latest notices before planning a visit. For the area, see the official guide to Tsukiji Outer Market.

Choose the seafood you came for.

Kaisendon · Seafood rice bowl

A kaisendon lets you try several kinds of seafood in one sitting. Many feature raw fish, with a different selection in each bowl. If you’ve been hoping to try uni (sea urchin) or ikura (salmon roe), look for a bowl that includes them. For variety, compare the mixed selections.

Planning lunch at a market or in a port town? Check the restaurant’s hours separately from the market’s. Some places finish serving earlier than you might expect. Knowing when to eat helps you decide which streets to explore first.

If you prefer your fish cooked, grilled eel is another donburi experience to consider.

Make time for the grill.

Unadon & unaju · Eel over rice

Unagi is freshwater eel. Prepared as kabayaki, it is grilled with a sweet, savoury glaze: fragrant at the surface, tender underneath. Over rice in a bowl, it’s unadon; served in a rectangular box, it’s unaju. The container alone doesn’t tell you the quality, so look at the portion and what comes with it.

At a specialist restaurant, choosing a smaller bowl or a generous box is part of the pleasure. Soup and other accompaniments may complete the meal. Let your appetite help you decide.

Some restaurants grill the eel after you order. Give this meal time of its own, with room for the journey from the station and any wait. Check reservations and last orders before you go, so you can settle in without watching the clock.

One more place you know you can eat.

Plan a favourite. Leave room for the day.

Before your trip, save one donburi restaurant you’re excited to try and find an easy beef bowl near your hotel. That gives you both something to look forward to and somewhere to turn when plans change.

On a busy day, follow a familiar sign. On another, try chicken and egg, or set off a little earlier for the tuna bowl you’ve been thinking about.

The next time you spot those Japanese characters on the street, they’ll mean something simple and useful: I could eat here.

Choosing a bowl that suits you

What if I prefer fully cooked fish or eggs?

Kaisendon often contains raw fish. Oyakodon and katsudon can have softly cooked egg, and an added egg may be raw. Check the description or ask staff about the preparation; the dish name alone won’t tell you how thoroughly everything is cooked.

What about vegetarian meals or dietary restrictions?

Gyudon contains beef; a typical katsudon contains pork; oyakodon contains chicken and egg. Even a vegetable tempura bowl may use fish in its sauce or stock. Check the restaurant’s ingredient and allergen information, and ask about your specific requirements rather than relying on the photo.
