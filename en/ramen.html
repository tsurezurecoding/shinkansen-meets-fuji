A BOWL ALONG THE WAY RAMEN IN JAPAN

Find your ramen. Meet Jiro.

What kind of bowl are you in the mood for?

A clear broth, the aroma of miso, the richness of pork bones. Noodles to dip, or noodles to mix. And perhaps a visit to Jiro for thick noodles and generous cuts of pork. Leave room in your Japan trip to find a bowl you love.

Ramen is on your list for Japan. You may already have a few restaurants saved, but a walk through the city brings more tempting bowls into view. One has a cloudy white broth, another a clear soup. The noodles, toppings and portions look different, too.

Those differences are part of the pleasure. A few clues on the menu will help you find the bowl you feel like eating today.

01 / A FEW CLUES ON THE MENU

Which flavor for your first bowl?

Shoyu, shio, miso and tonkotsu are names you will often see. You do not need to memorize a classification system. Think of them as clues to the aromas, textures and flavors you might enjoy.

SHOYU / SOY SAUCE

ShoyuFollow the aroma of soy sauce

Shoyu ramen is seasoned with soy sauce. The stock might be made with chicken, seafood, pork bones or a combination. The name can describe a clear, delicate bowl or something much richer.

At Bannai, pictured here, look for the clear soup and generous slices of roast pork. Kitakata Ramen Bannai (Japanese) ↗

SHIO / SALT

ShioNotice the broth and its aromas

Salt provides the seasoning, while each restaurant finds its own balance of chicken or seafood stock, aromatic oils, citrus and other ingredients. Shio does not automatically mean a light-tasting bowl.

Tempted by yuzu? At Menya Hyottoko in Tokyo’s Yurakucho district, Yuzu Yanagimen pairs Japanese-style dashi stock with the fragrance of this citrus fruit. Menya Hyottoko (Japanese) ↗

MISO / FERMENTED SOYBEAN PASTE

MisoFor warmth, aroma and depth

Miso brings a distinctive aroma and savory depth to the soup. Sapporo, in Hokkaido, is known for this style. After exploring the city on a cold day, a hot bowl can become a very good reason to choose your next stop.

For a taste of Sapporo, Sumire is a name to remember. Its official website lists restaurants to help you find one that fits your route. Sumire (Japanese) ↗

TONKOTSU / PORK BONE BROTH

TonkotsuThe savory depth of pork bones

Tonkotsu means a broth made from pork bones. You may recognize the cloudy, milky-looking soup and thin noodles associated with Hakata ramen. Its aroma and intensity vary from restaurant to restaurant.

At Ikkousha, pictured here, the fine foam on the surface is another distinctive feature. Hakata Ikkousha (Japanese) ↗

So why is there also “tonkotsu shoyu”?

Shoyu, shio and miso mainly describe the seasoning. Tonkotsu describes an ingredient used to make the stock. These names can overlap.

Compare Ikkousha’s white soup with Bannai’s clear broth in the photographs. They look very different, yet Bannai also describes drawing savory flavor from pork bones. Use names and photographs to choose, then enjoy discovering how the bowls actually taste.

02 / LET THE NOODLES LEAD YOUR NEXT MEAL

Will you dip, or mix?

Not every dish on a ramen menu arrives with its noodles in soup. With tsukemen, you dip them into a separate bowl of broth. With abura soba, you mix them into the sauce at the bottom. That first step is part of the enjoyment.

TSUKEMEN / DIPPING NOODLES

Tsukemen

A few noodles at a time, into the dipping broth.

The noodles and dipping broth arrive in separate bowls. Pick up a mouthful of noodles, dip them and eat. It is an appealing choice when you want to pay attention to the noodles’ texture.

Some restaurants, including Rokurinsha, rinse the cooked noodles in cold water before serving them. Cool noodles are intentional. Some shops also offer warm noodles, called atsumori.

Soup-wari, after the noodles
Where it is offered, you can ask for stock to be added to the remaining dipping broth, making it easier to drink. Ask if you would like to try it.

Rokurinsha’s menu and eating guide (Japanese) ↗

ABURA SOBA / SOUPLESS NOODLES

Abura soba

Bring the sauce up from the bottom and coat the noodles.

Abura soba is a dish of wheat noodles dressed with sauce and oil instead of soup. When it arrives, mix well from the bottom while the noodles are hot.

Some shops provide vinegar, chili oil or other condiments. Check the restaurant’s instructions, then adjust to your taste. A little seasoning can change the character of the bowl as you eat.

Despite “soba” in the name,
this dish generally uses the same Chinese-style wheat noodles as ramen, rather than buckwheat soba noodles.

Tokyo Aburagumi Sohonten’s eating guide (Japanese) ↗

ANOTHER KIND OF BOWL

Let vegetables take the lead.

Orange soup and colorful vegetables offer another way to choose. Soranoiro’s Veggie Soba, for example, makes vegetables part of the noodles, broth and toppings.

Sometimes a photograph is all it takes to decide what you want for lunch. If you have dietary requirements, check the description of the specific dish: “veggie” and “vegan” do not mean the same thing.

03 / MAKE ROOM IN YOUR TRIP FOR THIS BOWL

When you want noodles with a real chew.

Clear broth, fragrant miso, noodles to dip: there is plenty to explore already. But there is one more name worth getting to know: Ramen Jiro.

Thick slices of pork. Plenty of vegetables. Underneath, substantial noodles with a satisfying chew. Pork flavor and soy sauce meet in the broth, with garlic adding its aroma. Lift a few noodles, chew, then alternate with vegetables and pork. It is a different pleasure from a quick slurp of fine noodles.

The vegetable mound may catch your eye first in photographs. When choosing it as a travel meal, follow your appetite for those noodles, the pork or the garlic. Choose an amount that suits you and go for the pleasure of the bowl itself.

Ramen Jiro and Jiro-style restaurants

Ramen Jiro and restaurants described as “Jiro-style” are different brands. Butayama, for example, is a separate brand whose operator describes it as inspired by Jiro. When searching a map, check the restaurant’s name and its own information.

Butayama’s operator introduces the brand (Japanese) ↗

On your first visit, check the portion.

A bowl labeled “small” may still be a substantial meal. Butayama, for example, describes its small as roughly the size of a large serving at an ordinary ramen restaurant. That comparison is specific to Butayama, but the useful lesson is wider: do not judge the portion by its name alone.

Before choosing a large bowl or extra pork, check the basic noodle portion. If you would prefer fewer noodles, ask staff early, before preparation begins. Save enough appetite to enjoy the meal, rather than fitting it in immediately after a food crawl.

Butayama’s portions and ordering guide (Japanese) ↗

Four words for making it your own

You may encounter these terms at Jiro-style restaurants. The options and the right time to ask vary, so follow the instructions at the shop you visit.

Ninniku / garlic
Whether to add garlic, and how much.
Yasai / vegetables
The quantity of vegetables.
Abura / pork fat
Extra pork backfat or similar additions.
Karame / stronger seasoning
A more strongly seasoned broth. This refers to seasoning, not chili heat.

You do not need to increase everything on your first visit. Trying the standard bowl helps you learn what you would enjoy next time. Requests for fewer noodles and the final topping choices may be taken at different stages.

For examples of these terms and how to order, see Butayama’s official guide (Japanese). Its instructions are not a common procedure for every Ramen Jiro restaurant.

AFTER JIRO, OTHER BOWLS TO EXPLORE

Thick noodles and pork, with their own personalities.

Once you become interested in Jiro, you will also come across the terms “Jiro-kei” and “Jiro-inspired.” They are used for restaurants influenced by Jiro, among others. They do not mean that the shops all belong to one chain.

If you enjoy the combination of thick noodles, pork and vegetables, look at what other restaurants do with it. The cuts of meat, the appearance of the soup, a small bowl served on the side: even photographs offer clues to each restaurant’s character.

EDOMONDO

Edomondo

Those thick cuts of pork peeking through the vegetables make an inviting choice when you want to enjoy the meat as much as the noodles.

The Nishi-Nippori restaurant could fit a day of exploring Tokyo on the Yamanote loop line. There are also locations in Nakano and Shinbashi; use the official list to find one along your route.

Edomondo’s official restaurant links (Japanese) ↗

RAMEN BARIO

Ramen Bario

With large pieces of pork beside the vegetables, this bowl promises a hearty meal before you even lift the noodles.

Bario began in Tokyo’s Shinbashi district. Its official description highlights a rich pork bone broth and specially made noodles with a firm bite. It is another name to consider when you are in the mood for thick noodles.

Find the Shinbashi main restaurant on the map ↗

Check the details at each restaurant.

Bowls may look similar while their noodle portions and topping options differ. Before using a phrase you learned at Jiro, read the menu and instructions at the restaurant you are visiting.

04 / ONCE YOU HAVE CHOSEN YOUR RESTAURANT

From the doorway to the first bite.

When saving restaurants before your trip, check their official opening information as well as the address. Queues and sold-out dishes can happen at ramen shops of all kinds. Leave enough time before your next train or reservation.

If there is a machine by the entrance, check whether you order by buying a meal ticket. When there is a queue, signs should explain whether to buy the ticket first or wait until your turn. Follow the restaurant’s instructions, hand your ticket to staff and make any requests at the appropriate time.

If explaining your preferences in Japanese feels daunting, a restaurant such as Ichiran offers a written order form. Knowing how ordering works can be a good reason to choose a particular shop for your first meal.

When the bowl arrives, take care: it is hot. Slurping is common, but you do not have to imitate it. You do not have to finish all the soup, either. Once you have finished your meal, make room for the next person, especially when others are waiting.

Ichiran’s ordering guide / JNTO’s ramen guide

Find a bowl you will remember.

Yuzu today, miso in the next city. Tsukemen when you want to focus on the noodles; Jiro when you have an appetite for a hearty bowl.

Take a detour for a restaurant, or step into one on the way back to the station. Find a bowl you want to eat again, and you have one more reason to return to Japan.
