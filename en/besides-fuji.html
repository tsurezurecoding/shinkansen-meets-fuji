GREY SKY

Mt. Fuji is hidden. Everything else is still there.

You booked the right seat, you watched the right window at the right minute, and there was nothing but cloud. It is the most common way this ride disappoints people, and almost every guide stops writing at exactly this point.

Mt. Fuji is one of the 43 window views between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka. A grey sky takes that one. The castles, the lake the train runs across, the tea fields, the giant billboards nobody can explain and the yard full of sleeping Shinkansen do not depend on a hundred kilometres of clear air — several of them look better under cloud.

Why the near views survive. Mt. Fuji is roughly 40 km from the track, so it needs the whole sky to cooperate. A castle two hundred metres away needs nothing. That is the entire difference.

Before you give up on the mountain, note that it can appear from five separate points along the line, spread over more than an hour. Losing the famous one near Shin-Fuji does not mean losing all five.

Three that are better on a grey day

Lake Hamana — the train crosses the water on a low bridge with sky on both sides, and flat grey light makes the surface look like metal. It works from either side of the carriage, so no seat lottery.

The tea fields around Makinohara — rows of clipped green that get deeper and wetter-looking under cloud, not washed out like they are in hard noon sun.

Torikai depot, just before Shin-Osaka — rows of Shinkansen parked nose to tail. Weather is irrelevant; it is the last thing you see and most passengers miss it entirely because they have already packed up.

Was it ever going to be visible?

If you are reading this before you travel rather than after, the forecast page estimates whether Mt. Fuji is likely to show today, based on cloud around the summit rather than on the weather where you are standing. Clear winter mornings are far and away the best odds; a warm hazy afternoon in summer is the worst, even when it is not raining.
