EVERYDAY JAPAN · OMAMORI

Omamori: a small charm, a personal wish.

Omamori are charms received at Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples in Japan, carried close as a prayer for protection or a particular wish.

“May I finish my journey safely.” “May I do my best in my exams.” A small pouch can hold wishes like these.

01 · A wish to carry

A small pouch, entrusted with a wish

As you walk around Japanese shrines and temples, you may see rows of small, colorful pouches. These are omamori—a name connected to the Japanese word for protection.

People carry them close, or keep them in a bag, as they pray for health, safety, success in studies, or good relationships. Cloth pouches are familiar, but their forms vary by shrine and temple.

If one catches your eye, look at the words on it and wonder what wish it represents. Some people choose one for themselves; others give one to someone they care about.

02 · At a shrine or temple

Find one at a shrine or temple

Omamori are available at places such as a shrine or temple’s juyosho (授与所), the counter where sacred items are offered. A shrine is a place of Shinto worship; a temple is a place of Buddhist worship. Both may offer omamori.

  1. Pay your respects first

    Follow the site's guidance and offer a quiet prayer. Customs differ between shrines and temples.

  2. Choose a wish

    Read the words and descriptions at the juyosho (授与所), the counter where sacred items are offered. Ask if you are unsure.

  3. Offer the displayed amount

    At a shrine, the amount may be called hatsuho-ryo (初穂料). Names and amounts vary by place and item.

These items may be described as received or bestowed, rather than bought.

03 · Read the words

Read the words on the pouch

Similar colors and shapes can carry different wishes. Choose a word that catches your attention.

Choose a word to explore

交通安全

For a safe journey

A wish for safety in traffic and travel. It may be a helpful starting point when choosing something for a trip or everyday journeys.

These are general guides. The wishes and explanations can vary by shrine or temple, so look for its own information too.

04 · Keep it close

Carry it with care

People may wear an omamori or keep it in a bag they use often. If the place where you received it gives instructions for carrying or placing it, follow that guidance.

The cloth pouch is generally kept closed. To learn what it is for, read the words on the outside or check the shrine or temple's explanation.

A small gift for someone

“May your exam go well” or “May your journey be safe.” Share the meaning and the thought behind your choice, while respecting the other person's wishes and beliefs.

05 · When you return home

What happens after your trip?

An omamori may stay with you after you return home. If you are wondering what to do after a year, or cannot travel back to Japan, start by checking the guidance below.

A useful place to start

One year is not an expiry date that applies to every omamori.

Some people receive a new one around the New Year. Jinja Honcho says you may keep carrying an omamori until your wish comes true. Check the guidance from the place that offered yours.

What if I cannot return to Japan?

You do not necessarily need to let it go just because a year has passed. If you would like to return it, contact the shrine or temple where you received it. Some accept returns by post; confirm the address, eligible items, and process before sending anything.

Can I return it to a different shrine or temple?

Acceptance rules vary. Do not assume any nearby place—or a religious institution in your home country—will accept it. Check first with the place that offered it, and ask the other place if you plan to return it there.

Can I carry more than one omamori?

Jinja Honcho explains that there is no need to worry that carrying multiple omamori will make Shinto deities fight. For items from temples or other religions, check the guidance of those places too.

What if I do not know where it came from?

Look for a shrine or temple name on the pouch, its wrapping, or in travel photos. If you find one, the official website's omamori, old talismans, or return guidance may help.
