THE STORY

Two ways to arrive in Kyoto

Temples and traditional houses may be the buildings you first associate with Kyoto. At its railway gateway, a slender white tower and a vast roof of glass and metal offer a different view of the city. Both are part of the experience of arriving here.

Find the top of the tower from the train, look up into the station’s central space, then see the tower again from the street. Changing your distance and height reveals different relationships between the same buildings. There is a little architecture to explore before you set off for the temples.

BACKGROUND

A lighthouse for a city

Nidec Kyoto Tower stands opposite the station’s north, or Karasuma, side. It reaches 131 metres above ground. Its official account describes the white form as a lighthouse: the tiled roofs of the city were imagined as waves around it.

In the daytime photograph, the white shaft rises against the sky towards the circular observation room. Knowing the lighthouse idea invites you to look at the surrounding roofs as well as the tower itself.

After dark, the tower shines above the lights of the buildings and streets. Alongside its usual white illumination, coloured lighting is used for occasions including awareness campaigns. The official site gives information about the day’s lighting.

BACKGROUND

A smooth tower from 1964

The observation room opened on 28 December 1964, the year the Tokaido Shinkansen began running and Tokyo hosted the Olympic Games. Kyoto Central Post Office had previously occupied the site; its relocation opened the way for a new tourism and industry centre.

The architect was Mamoru Yamada, known for buildings that use curves. Instead of a conventional steel frame inside the tower, joined steel plates form a supporting shell. The uninterrupted white surface is connected to how the tower is built.

Look beyond the observation room to the long shaft below it. The tower presents a continuous surface, while the station roof reveals many individual members. Their different forms are a reason to look at both.

BACKGROUND

A valley under glass

The present station building opened in 1997 and is Kyoto’s fourth station building. Its architect was Hiroshi Hara. The official design account explains how Kyoto’s street grid informed the plan, with gateways corresponding to Karasuma and Muromachi streets.

The central concourse is imagined as a valley, with terraced spaces extending to the east and west. Glass and metal cover the central space. Follow the view from the people and walkways up to the roof and the scale of this indoor landscape becomes clearer.

A roof can disappear into the background when you are hurrying through. Pause to follow the lines of the structure and the daylight coming through the glass. This is another building shaped by ideas about Kyoto, before the temple visits begin.

BACKGROUND

See the same gateway from another height

Inside the station, there are places to change your viewpoint. Karasuma-koji Square on the fourth floor is one place to see the tower. The tenth-floor Skyway crosses between the east and west sides: a 185-metre passage at a height of 45 metres.

From the street you look up at the tower; from inside you look up at the roof; higher in the building you can look out across the northern cityscape. The relationship between tower and town changes as you move.

If you have time between journeys, the station itself can be a small place to explore. The official square guide lists locations and access hours.

BACKGROUND

From a brief window view to the Karasuma side

Look for Kyoto Tower on the Seat E side. In one position, its shaft and top rise above the station roof; in another, only the observation room and red-and-white tip appear over a hotel. Learning the shape of the top helps you find these brief views between buildings.

To walk towards the tower after leaving the train, head for the north, Karasuma or Central Entrance side. The Shinkansen is on the south side, connected by the north–south public passage. The tower’s official two-minute walk is measured from Kyoto Station’s north-side Central Entrance, rather than from the Shinkansen platform.

A tower glimpsed from the train becomes a building you can look up at from the street. On the other side of the train, Toji’s five-storey pagoda offers another Kyoto landmark. Its viewing position and photographs are covered in the separate Toji article.
